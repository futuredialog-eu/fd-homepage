**Residents do not experience their municipality through one channel. They read a post on Facebook, check the website for opening hours, pick up the local paper at the shop and ask a neighbour about the road works. For the person responsible for communication, every one of these touchpoints is a piece of the same puzzle – and in most local governments that person works alone or in a very small team.**

## Many touchpoints, one message

A resident's questions are simple: what is happening, how does it affect me and what should I do? The challenge is that the answer has to reach people of different ages, habits and levels of digital skill. A message that works for a young family may never reach a pensioner in a village, and vice versa. Each channel reaches a different audience, at a different speed and at a different cost.

## The municipal website

The website is the official source of information: decisions, services, contacts and forms.

- **Pros:** Under the municipality's full control. Permanent, searchable and suitable for detailed information.
- **Cons:** Passive – residents have to come looking. News is easily buried under menus, and keeping every page up to date takes time.

## Social media

Facebook groups and pages are often where local discussion actually happens.

- **Pros:** Fast, free and familiar to many residents. Allows two-way conversation and reaches people where they already spend time.
- **Cons:** Algorithms decide who sees a post. Not everyone uses social media, comments need moderation and misinformation spreads as quickly as facts. The municipality does not own the platform or the audience.

## The local newspaper

The municipal or regional newspaper is still a trusted source, especially for older residents.

- **Pros:** High credibility and good reach in rural areas. Suitable for longer explanations and background stories.
- **Cons:** Slow – a monthly issue cannot carry urgent news. Printing and distribution are expensive, and there is no feedback from readers.

## Newsletters and email

Email lists let the municipality reach residents who have chosen to follow it.

- **Pros:** Direct and inexpensive. Can be targeted to specific groups.
- **Cons:** Building a list takes effort and must comply with data protection rules. Messages compete with a full inbox and are often left unread.

## Notice boards and printed flyers

Notice boards at the shop, library or bus stop remain part of village life.

- **Pros:** Reach residents who are not online. Visible and local.
- **Cons:** Limited space, no way to measure reach and easily outdated.

## Phone, front desk and public meetings

Personal contact is where residents bring their real questions and concerns.

- **Pros:** Builds trust. Allows complex issues to be explained and gives immediate feedback.
- **Cons:** Time-consuming for staff and does not scale. Meetings often attract the same small group of people.

## The real challenge: keeping the pieces together

Each channel on its own is manageable. The difficulty is running all of them at once. The same announcement has to be rewritten for the website, Facebook, the newspaper and the notice board. Information drifts out of sync, and an urgent message – a water outage or a storm warning – has to go out everywhere within minutes. Feedback arrives scattered across comments, emails and phone calls, with no single overview.

A sustainable approach starts with a clear plan: deciding which channel is the primary source, which channels point back to it and who is responsible for each. Tools that publish to several channels at once and collect feedback in one place save time and reduce mistakes. A community app can serve as that hub, giving residents one reliable place for news, alerts and feedback while the familiar channels continue to do their part. When the pieces fit together, residents get a consistent message – and the communication team gets time back for the work that matters.
